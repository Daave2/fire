Learnings & The Future

What This Experience Has Shown

The Waterloo Road fire exposed more than the immediate challenge of evacuation. It revealed how fragile the recovery process can be when communication is fragmented, homes are insecure, habitability is unclear, and displaced residents are left to navigate insurers, utilities, accommodation, and public bodies largely on their own.

A Community Already Stretched

To understand why this fire hit so hard, you have to understand the area. South Shore sits within Blackpool, a borough consistently ranked among the most deprived local authority areas in England. Many residents here are on low incomes, in insecure housing, or dealing with health conditions that make disruption harder to absorb.

But deprivation statistics don't tell the whole story. What they miss is the strength of the community underneath. The neighbours who watch each other's houses. The café that feeds people who can't always afford to eat. The WhatsApp group that formed overnight when official channels failed. South Shore has always looked after its own — not because the system works, but often because it doesn't.

There is a hope, shared by residents and local representatives alike, that this tragedy might become a catalyst for the investment and attention this area has long needed. Not just the cleared site, but the streets around it — the housing, the infrastructure, the services. South Shore deserves more than emergency responses. It deserves the kind of sustained support that prevents emergencies in the first place.

A Street With a Longer Story

Waterloo Road was never just a backdrop to the fire. Blackpool Council's heritage record says South Shore was known in the early 19th century as New Blackpool, a separate community and rival resort to Blackpool. Lytham Road, then Broad Lane, and Waterloo Road became principal routes through that early settlement.

The council also describes Waterloo Road as one of South Shore's first east-west streets, shown on the 1838 Tithe map. By the late 19th century it rivalled Bond Street as a main commercial street, and by 1938 it had shops including Woolworths, Saxone and Boots, alongside independent traders, a cinema, a bank and a post office. The same record notes later deterioration in the fabric of Waterloo Road and Bond Street, while also recognising surviving historic character.

That history changes how the fire should be understood. This was not an isolated commercial block behind some houses. It was part of a local high street with a long civic, retail and community role. Recovery should therefore mean more than clearing rubble. It should ask what kind of street Waterloo Road is becoming, and whether residents closest to the damage have a real voice in that future.

What Went Right

It's important to acknowledge what worked. The emergency response itself was effective. Fifteen fire engines, aerial ladder platforms, a drone unit, and specialist crews tackled a complex blaze over four days. No injuries were reported. That is a significant achievement and a credit to the Lancashire Fire and Rescue Service.

The immediate evacuation was handled decisively. Police were at the door within minutes. Residents were directed to safety. A temporary shelter was set up at a local pub. In the acute emergency phase, the system worked as it should.

And the community response was extraordinary. Neighbours kept watch. Strangers donated essentials. A WhatsApp group became a lifeline. The "Rebuild Waterloo" Facebook group coordinated donations, fundraising, and moral support with remarkable efficiency. People stepped up without being asked.

What Didn't

The failures were not in the emergency itself — they were in everything that came after. From the moment the fire brigade handed the site to the council, a gap opened up that residents fell through. The key failings can be summarised under five headings:

1. Communication

Displaced residents had no named point of contact, no communication channel, and no regular updates for 12 days. Information that directly affected them — such as a wall collapsing into their property — was not communicated at all. They found out from photos shared online.

Even after community-organised meetings on February 11 and February 18, residents were still waiting for a formal coordinator. Communication remained fragmented across Building Control, Housing Options, and other departments, with no single thread tying them together.

2. Security

Residents were told to leave their front doors unlocked for emergency access. When the fire brigade handed the site to the council, those properties were left unsecured and unmonitored. It took a resident reporting children on the demolition site before 24/7 security was established.

Even then, the perimeter remained porous. On February 27, three weeks after the fire, Niki Cooke returned to find the back door knocked down and the property completely compromised by a break-in. Lancashire Police are investigating. Residents believe the failure to properly secure evacuated properties contributed to the precise outcome they had feared.

3. Administrative Burden

Displaced residents were left to individually contact every utility provider, insurer, employer, and government department to explain their situation. United Utilities sent a debt collection threat while Niki was displaced. Council tax, water, gas, electric, broadband — each required separate calls, often without the account numbers locked inside inaccessible homes.

A single coordinated notification to service providers — confirming displacement and requesting account holds — would have prevented hours of phone calls, credit score threats, and unnecessary distress.

4. Parity

Even the limited communication that existed was not equally distributed. Businesses appeared to receive more attention than displaced families. This may not have been intentional — but the effect was that the people most affected had the least information.

5. Recovery Coordination

Even once emergency access issues eased, residents still faced a maze of unresolved practical problems: securing damaged homes, understanding whether they were actually habitable, coordinating structural surveys, managing alternative accommodation, dealing with utilities, and navigating insurance decisions about cleaning versus replacement. These were not isolated inconveniences. Together, they defined whether recovery was realistic or simply nominal.

What was missing was a joined-up recovery process. The end of the cordon should not have marked the end of coordinated support. For residents closest to the fire, that was when a different kind of support was needed most.

By April, a sixth failing had become clear: record keeping and transparency. Residents were still trying to understand who held responsibility for the remaining site, what structural reports were outstanding, why the Waterloo Road end remained blocked, what had been decided about fencing and rear access, and what needed to happen next. When people have to use FOI/EIR routes to reconstruct decisions affecting their homes, the routine communication process has already failed.

What Should Change

None of these are radical proposals. They are practical, low-cost steps that any local authority could implement:

  • Immediate handover notification: When site control passes from emergency services to the council, affected residents should be informed the same day — by phone, text, or in person.
  • Named coordinator within 48 hours: One person, one phone number, available as a single point of contact for all displaced households.
  • Daily status updates: Even "no change today" is better than silence. A simple group text or email chain costs nothing and prevents speculation.
  • Utility notification service: A single letter or call from the council to utility providers confirming displacement, preventing bills, threats, and credit damage.
  • Security by default: If residents are instructed to leave doors unlocked, security must be established immediately — not after an incident is reported.
  • Equal access to information: Residents and businesses should receive the same updates through the same channels.
  • Resident displacement pack: A simple printed pack given at the point of evacuation, containing: who to contact, what to do about utilities, how to start an insurance claim, and where to get emergency support.
  • Habitability clarity: Residents need a clear written distinction between being allowed access and a property being genuinely safe and practical to live in.
  • Recovery case management: In prolonged incidents, support should continue beyond the emergency phase to help residents navigate repairs, utilities, temporary accommodation, and insurance-related evidence needs.
  • Practical living standards: The loss of plumbing, washing facilities, secure doors, weatherproof windows, or safe rear access should be treated as central recovery issues, not side notes.
  • Written recovery log: Decisions about dangerous structures, access, hoarding, demolition, fencing and street cleansing should be logged and shared with affected residents in a form they can rely on.
  • Hardship route for council tax: Where a home is unusable after a major incident, residents should be given a clear discretionary relief route immediately, rather than being told to wait for separate valuation processes while bills continue.

The Wider Context

Separate from the cause of the fire, residents were also raising wider concerns about neighbourhood safety and site security. Because the confirmed cause has not been published and police have stated that the fire is not being treated as suspicious, this site does not treat those concerns as evidence about how the fire started.

Their relevance is narrower but still important: they help explain why residents were alarmed by unlocked homes, delayed access, and an accessible demolition perimeter after the evacuation.

The Future of Waterloo Road

The Smart Mart site is now a cleared plot. Residents were told at a community meeting that the council may look at purchasing the land. Councillors and residents have discussed the possibility of a community green space — benches, flowers, perhaps a "chatty bench" where people can stop and talk. No public source reviewed for this site confirms an adopted plan for the cleared plot, so that idea is recorded here as a resident and councillor discussion rather than a settled decision.

"If we get a lovely green space there, with benches and flowers, it will be so much nicer than the view we all had." — A neighbour, Gordon Street

What Is Already in Motion

Public sources point to a wider regeneration context around South Shore, even though they do not amount to a confirmed scheme for the Smart Mart plot itself.

  • GOV.UK records a £1.5 million Pride in Place Impact Fund allocation for Blackpool, intended for community spaces, public spaces and high street or town centre revitalisation.
  • Chris Webb MP has publicly called for that funding to be used in South Shore, including Waterloo Road, Bond Street and Lytham Road, and has said that a council masterplan and an Empowerment-led People's Plan are part of the foundation for local regeneration.
  • Public reporting says South Shore has already seen planting, benches, verge improvements, a planned mural and deep-cleaning activity around Waterloo Road and Lytham Road through related public realm work.
  • ITV News reported in March 2026 that Counselling In The Community's Waterloo Road hub had received £40,000 for repairs, with further money earmarked toward purchase of the premises, and that the reopened hub is expected to include improved accessibility, counselling suites and shared community space.
  • Blackpool Council's South Shore Stories project is collecting memories, photographs and artefacts, with an exhibition and commemorative book planned for 2026. That matters because regeneration is not only about land use; it is also about what people remember and value.

Examples That Could Translate

The strongest examples are not grand, expensive masterplans. They are smaller projects that make neglected land useful, visible, maintained and shaped by people who live nearby.

1. A pocket park with a maintenance plan

Historic England's Lowestoft case study is especially relevant. An overgrown corner site that attracted fly-tipping became Cleveland Road Pocket Park after a community group, council and local partners secured access, added planting, created a mural and agreed ongoing care. The lesson for Waterloo Road is practical: a green space only works if ownership or access, safety, planting, lighting, volunteer capacity and long-term maintenance are settled at the start.

2. Public space that can host community life

Historic England's Cullompton case study shows how public realm work can create a small civic space for cultural events, markets and everyday gathering. A Waterloo Road version would not need to be elaborate: benches, planting, clear sightlines, good drainage, litter management, space for a small community notice board and occasional local activity would already be a meaningful change from boarded emptiness.

3. Storytelling as part of the repair

Northallerton's heritage trail used digital storytelling to bring local history into the high street. South Shore already has its own version beginning through South Shore Stories. A future Waterloo Road project could include simple history panels, QR-linked resident memories, or artwork about the street's older life: Lido Baths, Bond Street shops, Waterloo Road traders, guest houses, beach days and the people who kept the area going.

4. Action on empty or stubborn premises

For the wider Waterloo Road and Bond Street area, vacant and underused buildings remain part of the story. GOV.UK's High Street Rental Auction guidance shows one national tool councils can use for commercial premises that have been empty for long periods. That is not a direct answer for a demolished plot, but it is relevant to the surrounding high street: recovery should not leave nearby buildings to decline until the next crisis.

A Practical Ask

For residents, the fairest next step would be a published options note for the cleared site and the Waterloo Road end of Gordon Street. It should explain who owns or controls the land, what safety or structural issues remain, what interim uses are possible, what funding routes might apply, how maintenance would be handled, and how residents can feed in before decisions are made.

For the residents most directly affected, the immediate future became less about crossing the threshold and more about whether home could actually function as home again. Even once access was technically restored, some properties remained not properly livable — with broken windows, leaking roofs, interrupted services, security concerns, and ongoing uncertainty around repair scope and timing. Being "allowed home" and actually being able to live there were two very different things.

As of mid-April, residents were still waiting for clearer answers about the Waterloo Road end of Gordon Street, including structural reports for the flat/building at the end of the street and the steps required before normal access could resume. Any future for the cleared site has to start with the people who live next to it being kept informed, protected and involved.

Why This Site Exists

This site was not created to sensationalise or to point fingers. It exists because the experience of the Gordon Street residents deserves to be recorded — accurately, fairly, and in their own words where possible.

If it helps one council improve their emergency displacement process, if it gives one future evacuee a sense that they're not alone, or if it simply ensures that this community's story is not forgotten when the news cycle moves on — then it has served its purpose.
